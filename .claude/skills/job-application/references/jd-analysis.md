# Decoding a Job Description

## What a job ad actually is

A job ad is three documents fused together and written by three different people:

1. **HR's compliance layer** — the boilerplate, the EEO statement, the "5+ years experience" that legal wanted. Mostly noise.
2. **The hiring manager's real wishlist** — usually 3–5 items, often buried in the middle, sometimes written in a noticeably different register from the surrounding text. This is the signal.
3. **The employer brand pitch** — "we're a fast-growing team of passionate..." Skim it, but note the vocabulary; echoing their language back in the letter shows you read it.

Your job is to find layer 2. It's usually the responsibilities section, and specifically the responsibilities that are described with unusual specificity. Generic responsibilities are copy-paste; specific ones are someone describing an actual problem on their desk.

## Reading the pain

Roles are created because of one of four situations. Identify which:

- **Growth** — the team is scaling and needs more hands. Ad emphasises volume, delivery, execution. They want reliability.
- **Gap** — a capability doesn't exist yet. Ad emphasises building, establishing, defining, "first hire in this function." They want someone who works without scaffolding.
- **Replacement** — someone left. Ad is often the old JD lightly edited, reads slightly stale. Look for what's been added to the old template — that's what went wrong last time.
- **Transformation** — a change programme needs a driver. Ad emphasises change management, stakeholder influence, "partner with." They want political skill as much as craft.

Each calls for a different cover letter spine. A Gap role wants evidence you've built from zero. A Transformation role wants evidence you've moved people who didn't report to you.

## Repeated words are load-bearing

If a phrase appears three or more times across the ad, it's the thing. It's not padding — it's what the hiring manager keeps thinking about. Count them. Whatever tops the count belongs in the first paragraph of the cover letter.

## Seniority calibration by verb

| Verb family | Level being described |
|---|---|
| support, assist, contribute to, help | junior |
| execute, deliver, manage, maintain | mid |
| own, lead, design, define | senior |
| shape, set direction, establish, influence org-wide | staff / principal / lead |

A mismatch between the title and the verbs is worth flagging. "Senior" attached to execution verbs often means an inflated title and a mid-level scope — and probably a mid-level band.

## Requirements are a wishlist, not a gate

Standard guidance holds: the listed requirements describe an ideal candidate who does not exist. Meeting roughly 60% of must-haves is a legitimate application. But this cuts both ways — a candidate meeting 30% is spending hours on a lottery ticket, and it's kinder to say so than to help them write a beautiful letter for a role they won't get.

The exceptions that *are* hard gates: specific certifications and licences, legal work authorisation, security clearances, and named tool experience where the tool is the entire job.

## Red flags worth naming to the user

- No salary band in a jurisdiction where disclosure is common or required
- Two jobs in one ad (e.g. "instructional design and full-stack development and data analysis")
- "Wear many hats," "thrive in chaos," "we work hard and play hard" — under-resourcing, stated politely
- The role has been reposted repeatedly (check the posting date and any repost signals)
- Title downgrade relative to the user's current scope, even with a pay bump
- Requirements that contradict each other — a sign nobody senior has scoped the role

Say these plainly rather than burying them. The user can decide the tradeoff is worth it, but they should decide with the information.

## What to hand back

A filled requirements table, the pain diagnosis (which of the four situations), the top repeated terms, and the seniority read. Keep it to one screen. The analysis serves the application; it isn't the deliverable.
